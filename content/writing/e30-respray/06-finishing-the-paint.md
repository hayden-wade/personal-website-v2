# Finishing the paint

## Fresh paint is not automatically finished paint

When the E30 came back from the painter it was shiny enough that, from a few metres away, it looked finished. Up close, the clear coat told a different story.

There was a fair amount of orange peel because the clear had been applied heavily. There were also local runs and build-ups around some recesses. None of that undid the transformation, but it meant the surface did not yet have the flat, mirror-like finish that a really good respray can have.

The positive side of having a decent amount of clear on the car is that there is material available for correction. The negative side is that flattening clear is slow and carries risk: if you sand through an edge or high spot, you can turn a cosmetic finishing job into a paint repair very quickly.

## My plan for correction

This is one part of the project where I would rather be honest than write a fake retrospective. The paint has held up extremely well, but the full wet-sand/cut/polish process is not a chapter I consider completely finished even now. The car still deserves a careful final correction before I eventually sell it.

The approach I would take is conservative: inspect the clear thickness where possible, identify and carefully level only the defects that justify sanding, protect edges/body lines, then move through a controlled wet-sanding and machine-polishing process. I would rather leave a small amount of texture than chase absolute flatness and break through the clear.

On broad, safe panels such as the bonnet, roof and boot, the aim would be to reduce the obvious texture and then compound and polish back to gloss. Around sharp edges, swage lines, badge recesses and panel corners I would be much more cautious.

## What I learned about tools

This project repeatedly taught me that buying the correct tool can be cheaper than trying to save money.

During bodywork I generated huge amounts of dust because I did not have professional extraction. I also spent more hours than necessary sanding filler because my blocks and methods were basic. If I started another full respray today, I would buy or borrow a proper long-block sanding system with dust extraction and use a good workshop vacuum from the beginning.

The same principle applies to final paint correction. A quality dual-action or rotary polisher, suitable pads, good lighting and proper abrasives are much cheaper than repainting a panel because I rushed with the wrong equipment.

I did the E30 in a cramped outdoor courtyard because that was what I had. It worked, but a clean enclosed garage would have saved an enormous amount of time. No rain on bare metal. No flash rust. Better lighting. Less dirt. Less dust blowing into everything. The environment matters almost as much as the products.

## Would I skim-coat a whole hail-damaged car again?

Probably not – at least not in exactly the same way.

The skim-coat approach did work. Years later the filler has held up and the panels have not started cracking just because the car sat in the sun. But it was incredibly labour-intensive, especially because I was learning as I went and often put filler on thicker than a professional would.

If I were faced with an E30 covered in hail damage today, I would first ask whether paintless dent repair, metal finishing, panel replacement or a combination of methods could reduce the amount of filler required. I would also question whether the particular car was worth the hundreds of personal hours involved.

That does not mean I regret doing it. The whole point, in a way, was that I did not know how to do any of this when I started. By the end I understood body filler, block sanding, rust repair, basic welding, primer preparation, trim removal and the sheer amount of labour hiding underneath a decent paint job.

I effectively paid for that education in weekends and sandpaper.
