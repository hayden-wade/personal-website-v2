# Repairing the hail damage

## Learning by sanding

Once the car was stripped, I started sanding. I had an orbital sander, a pile of discs, hand-sanding paper and the confidence that comes from having watched enough YouTube videos to think you know what you are doing.

I initially worked with something around 240 grit, but I quickly became impatient with how slowly it was cutting. I moved down to roughly 120 grit in a lot of areas and worked panel by panel. The goal was not to grind the whole car to shiny bare steel; I wanted to remove failed clear, key the existing finish properly and expose the areas that actually needed repair.

The hail dents made the job more awkward. I could not simply run the orbital over everything. I had to hand-sand inside individual depressions and around the tighter shapes of the front and rear valances. The front valance in particular seemed to have endless little corners where an orbital sander could not reach.

The plastic bumpers were sanded more gently, and I removed their black moulding so the painter could refinish those pieces separately. One of the bumper sections had also cracked from the storm damage, which I left for the painter to repair later with proper plastic-repair material.

Working outside made all of this far more annoying than it needed to be. I was living in West Croydon in an older house with a very narrow single garage. There was technically a garage, but there was nowhere near enough room to work comfortably around a car inside it. I drove the BMW through to an open courtyard area behind the garage and did most of the bodywork there.

There were no convenient power points, so extension leads became part of the workshop. Lighting was makeshift. I could not make unlimited noise at night because the neighbour’s fence was right beside me. Most importantly, the car was exposed to weather. Any time I uncovered bare steel I had to think about protecting it, because a bit of rain or overnight moisture could give me flash rust and create more work.

## My first attempt at filler

Then came body filler – or “bog”, depending on how Australian the conversation is.

My first filler came from Supercheap Auto. I did not get along with it at all. It seemed to harden too quickly for my skill level, it did not spread as smoothly as I wanted, and I spent too much time fighting the material instead of learning how to shape the panel.

Part-way through the job I discovered an automotive paint-supply shop close to home around West Croydon. That was one of the biggest quality-of-life improvements in the project. The filler was better, the sandpaper was cheaper, and the staff sold products intended for this kind of work rather than the small general-purpose quantities I had been buying elsewhere.

At first I treated each hail dent individually. I would spread filler perhaps 100 mm or so around a dent, sand it, feel it, look at it and think I had fixed it. Then I would put a torch down low across the panel at night and see waves, shallow depressions and transitions everywhere.

That low-angle light was brutal, but useful.

## The skim-coat decision

The boot was where I really started to understand the problem. I spent hours filling and sanding individual spots but could still see imperfections across the panel. The bonnet went much the same way. Eventually I got sick of chasing one little dent at a time and changed approach.

I skim-coated much larger areas – in some cases effectively the whole panel.

That meant spreading a thin layer of filler across the bonnet, sanding it flat, inspecting it, filling again where necessary, and repeating the process. I did the boot in a similar way, then moved on to the roof and doors. The roof took much longer simply because it was such a large surface and every small wave became visible once you started trying to make it genuinely straight.

Because I was inexperienced, I often applied the filler thicker than I needed to. That created another problem: a truly ridiculous amount of sanding. I used coarse paper to knock it down, then progressively finer grades as the shape improved. Dust ended up everywhere.

I did wear a proper respirator, but if I did the job again I would invest in a professional long sanding block and proper dust extraction from day one. A vacuum-assisted block and a decent extractor would have made the job cleaner and, more importantly, would probably have helped me get the panels flatter with less work.

## Fixing old dents while I was there

The hail damage was not the only bodywork. One door had a dent from an earlier incident where the car slipped while I was jacking it up. There were also a few smaller dents left by previous owners. Since the car was already stripped and covered in filler dust, it made sense to fix those at the same time.

I also repaired a dent in the black window-surround area with filler. It may not have been the most textbook repair in the world, but after shaping and sanding it came out surprisingly well.

At this stage I started using aerosol epoxy primer on exposed metal and completed repairs to keep them sealed, particularly because the car lived outside. It was never ideal having to prime a section simply because rain might arrive before I got back to it, but that was the reality of doing the project without a proper workshop.

## Glaze putty: the product I wish I had discovered earlier

Late in the bodywork process, the paint supplier recommended glazing putty for the fine imperfections. That was a revelation.

Before that, if I found a tiny low spot or pinhole I felt like I was constantly stepping backwards: roughing an area up again, mixing another batch of filler, spreading it, sanding it, and potentially disturbing work I had already finished. The glaze putty let me address small surface imperfections with a much thinner application.

It made the final stages faster and much less frustrating. I could work my way around the car, use low-angle light to identify defects, skim those tiny areas and keep refining the surface without turning every small imperfection into another major repair.

By the end, the E30 looked ridiculous – patches of old blue, primer, filler and sand-throughs everywhere – but when I ran my hand over the major panels, they were finally starting to feel straight.
