# Rust repair, final prep & primer

## The hidden windscreen rust

The most important rust repair revealed itself when I removed the front windscreen. In one lower corner of the aperture, the seal had been compromised and water had clearly been getting in for a long time. Once the rubber was out, I could see that this was more than a light surface stain.

I cleaned the area back with a wire wheel and decided the affected steel needed to come out. I cut the rust into a simple square/rectangular shape so I could fabricate a replacement patch without making life unnecessarily complicated.

I had bought a small gasless MIG welder for jobs like this. In hindsight, a gas-shielded MIG would have been much nicer to use. The little gasless machine was messy and produced more spatter, but it was enough to teach me the basics and get the patch in.

I shaped a small piece of replacement steel, positioned it in the opening and welded it into the windscreen frame. I was careful not to simply run one long bead and pour heat into the surrounding panel. Once it was secure, I dressed the welds back and finished the area so the windscreen seal would eventually have a clean surface to sit against.

## The boot-seal rust – and a lesson from 2026

There was also rust around the rear boot-seal/pinch-weld area. At the time it looked much more superficial. I attacked it with a wire wheel on a drill, cleaned as much corrosion as I could reach and refinished it.

It looked fine for years, but this is one of the places where hindsight matters. In 2026 I found rust returning in that section, and I am now repairing it more thoroughly by drilling spot welds and getting into the affected metal properly.

That does not mean the original repair was pointless; it is actually one of the most useful lessons from the whole project. If rust is buried inside a seam or pinch weld, making the visible surface shiny is not necessarily the same as removing the corrosion. If I were doing that section again, I would spend more time opening the area up and making sure the rust was gone from both sides before paint ever went near it.

## Small fabrication jobs along the way

Because I already had the welder out, the respray turned into an excuse to fix a few unrelated things. The old exhaust tip had rusted away and a previous owner had fitted a fairly dodgy clamped section. I bought a length of roughly 2-inch exhaust tube online, cut it to suit and welded on a cleaner replacement tip. It was a small job, but it made the back of the car look much more complete.

I also bought an aftermarket front lip and side skirts from Mr Bodykits and test-fitted them before paint. They looked good in concept, but the fit taught me another lesson: aftermarket body pieces can be cheap for a reason. The side skirts wanted to pull away from the body and eventually needed screws plus Sikaflex to hold them close. The front lip also never sat quite as naturally as an OEM part would have.

If I were doing the same car again today, I would probably spend more money and source original/OEM-style pieces rather than fighting the fitment.

## Getting the car genuinely ready

By this point I had spent months going around the same panels. Fill. Sand. Inspect. Prime an exposed section. Find another tiny defect. Add glaze. Sand again. Repeat.

I eventually found a painter through Facebook Marketplace who agreed to spray the car for around $2,000 in labour, with me supplying the paint and doing the overwhelming majority of the preparation. He came to inspect the car before the job and pointed out a few areas he wanted me to improve. That was reassuring because, overall, he thought the preparation was good – particularly for someone doing their first full car.

I was also under time pressure. I was preparing to move from Adelaide to Sydney near the end of 2023, and by around October I could feel the calendar closing in. Ideally, I would have taken another month and obsessed over every panel. In reality, I had to decide when “good” was good enough and get the shell to the painter.

## Primer at the painter

The car was transported to the painter and we went over the surface again together, sanding and preparing it for primer. He repaired the cracked bumper properly with plastic repair material and corrected a couple of remaining areas with high-build/UV-curing or similar surfacing products where required.

The first full primer stage was a huge psychological milestone. For months I had been looking at a patchwork shell. Once the E30 was one consistent light colour, I could suddenly see the car as a complete shape again.

We allowed the primer to cure, then came back and sanded the whole car again for the final paint process. Orbital sanders helped speed up the large areas, with hand work around edges and details.

In hindsight, this is one stage I would change. If I were repeating the project with more time, I would use the opportunity for a more deliberate high-build primer and block-sanding stage. A proper high-build surface gives you one more chance to chase tiny waves before colour goes on. We were working to a deadline, and the system we used worked, but I now understand why professional restorations spend so much time at this stage.

At this point, after months of beige/grey filler dust, the next colour the car was going to become was finally Glacier Blue again.
