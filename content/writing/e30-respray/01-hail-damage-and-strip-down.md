# Why I resprayed my E30

## Before the E30

My first car was a GQ Nissan Patrol with an RB30. It was a genuinely good car: straight, tidy, a surprisingly nice interior for its age, and a lot of fun once I fitted a turbo setup. The problem was that I had also tuned it for 98 octane and was driving it to work every day. I had built myself a heavy four-wheel-drive that drank fuel, and I barely took it off-road.

Eventually I decided it made more sense to sell it. I still have a soft spot for that Patrol, but at the time I wanted something smaller, cheaper to run and a bit more interesting as a road car. I briefly ended up in a very cheap Ford Festiva while I worked out what came next. It was memorable for all the wrong reasons: a loud exhaust, a pretty ordinary interior, and on hot days an inexplicable smell that I can still remember. It did its job, but I definitely wanted something better.

I had started becoming more interested in older European cars. My family is German, so perhaps there was a bit of that in it, but more than anything I loved the boxy design language of 1980s cars. The E30 stood out. They were simple, handsome, had a huge enthusiast community, and even then there seemed to be a healthy supply of parts and information online.

## Buying the E30

In 2020 I found a Glacier Blue 1990 BMW E30 318i sedan advertised for about $4,000. I went to inspect it and one small thing immediately stuck with me: the doors. Closing one gave that solid old-BMW thud that made the car feel much more substantial than I expected. Compared with the Patrol, which always felt a little tinny in places, the E30 felt like a quality object even though it was already around thirty years old.

It was not perfect. The paint on the roof was peeling, there appeared to have been some previous paintwork around the rear of the car, there were small dents and age-related marks, and the seats were not immaculate. But it was a solid car and, looking back, probably a better starting point than either of the E30 coupes I later bought.

The 318i was not fast. That was never really the point. It was light, simple, fun to drive and full of character, so I used it as my daily for the next year or so.

## 28 October 2021

Then came the hailstorm.

I was at work in Elizabeth, in Adelaide’s northern suburbs, when I heard the storm hitting the building. It was incredibly loud. I walked out under the covered area and looked across the car park as large hailstones smashed into the cars outside. At first I actually had a little bit of hope. The E30 was old, the steel felt thick, and I remember thinking that maybe it would get away with it.

It did not.

When the storm eased and I walked out to the car, my heart sank. The bonnet was covered in dents. The roof and boot were bad. One side of the car had escaped a little better because the hail had come through at an angle, but the opposite side had taken plenty of hits. One hailstone caught the edge of the windscreen and cracked it. The rest of the glass survived, which in hindsight was at least one small win.

## Written off – sort of

I took the car to Budget Direct’s assessment process a few days later. Their conclusion was straightforward: it was a write-off.

Initially I was told I could not retain the car, which I was not very happy about. It was an old BMW, but by then E30s had clearly become enthusiast cars and I knew that treating it like a disposable old sedan did not really reflect what it meant to me or what similar cars were becoming worth. After some calls and discussion about the car’s value and classic status, I was eventually able to keep it.

From memory, the policy value was around $4,000 and I received roughly $2,000 while retaining the car. It was not a huge financial windfall, but at least the E30 stayed with me.

For a while, though, I had no idea what to do with it.

## The almost-sale that changed my mind

The damaged car sat for months. At one stage I decided I should just sell it. A woman and her boyfriend came to inspect it; he was a mechanic, and the two of them were genuinely excited by the car. They looked past the hail damage and kept talking about how good the underlying E30 was and how much they wanted it.

Strangely, their enthusiasm talked me out of selling it.

While they were standing there telling me why they wanted the car, I started thinking the same thing: they were right. Underneath the dents, it was still a nice E30. I backed out of the sale and kept it.

That decision meant the car sat for quite a bit longer while I built up the motivation to do something about it. By late 2022 into 2023 I had finally made up my mind: rather than paying someone to do everything, I was going to learn how to prepare the body myself.

## Stripping it down

I started by watching a lot of YouTube videos, reading forums and working out the basic process: disassembly, sanding, rust repair, filler, primer, more sanding, and eventually paint.

The first physical job was stripping the exterior. I removed the front and rear windscreens and their lock strips, bumpers, bumper mouldings, mirrors, grilles, wipers, cowl plastics, badges, door handles, side mouldings, window trim, chrome trim and the small exterior pieces that would either get in the way or leave an ugly masking line if I painted around them.

Some parts were easy. Others were classic old-car work: brittle clips, trim that did not want to move, and a lot of cautious levering while trying not to damage something that might be difficult or expensive to replace. The chrome strips along the doors were particularly frustrating. I did slightly bend and mark some of them during removal, although they eventually went back on acceptably.

I bagged the fasteners and small parts in labelled zip-lock bags and put the larger components into plastic tubs. That bit of organisation ended up being worth the effort. Months later, when it was time to put the car back together in a hurry, I was very glad I had not thrown every screw and clip into one mystery bucket.

Removing the front windscreen also revealed the first extra problem. In the lower corner of the windscreen aperture, water had clearly been getting through an old seal and there was a real rust spot hiding underneath. What had started as a hail-repair respray was already turning into a more complete body project.

And that was before I had sanded a single panel.
