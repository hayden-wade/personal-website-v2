# The finished E30

## A project that took over 2023

The respray absorbed a huge amount of my spare time in 2023. It was evenings after work, weekends, dusty clothes, filler on everything, trips to the paint supplier and the repetitive feeling of sanding a panel I thought I had already finished.

Near the end, the pressure increased because I was preparing to move interstate. I would have liked more time for final body refinement, but eventually a project needs to move forward. I had to trust the work I had done and send the car to paint.

The painter’s reaction meant a lot to me. He told me the preparation was very good for a first-timer and that most people do not bring a DIY-prepared car to him at that level. It was not perfect, but the panels I had spent the most time on were genuinely straight and ready.

## Seeing it in sunlight

The first time I saw the assembled car outside in proper sunlight was one of the best moments of the project.

In some booth photos Glacier Blue looked almost grey. Outdoors the colour came alive again. The paint was glossy, the glass was back in, the black trim looked fresh, and all of those individual tubs of parts had returned to the car.

I remember a sense of relief as much as excitement. I had actually followed the project through. There were many points where selling the dented car or paying someone else to take over would have been easier. Instead, I had learned the process and done the vast majority of the dirty work myself.

My roommate at the time was impressed with it too, which helped – after living around the project for so long, it was nice to see someone else react to the finished result rather than another grey panel covered in sanding marks.

## Was it financially worth it?

If the only goal was to maximise the value of an E30 318i sedan, probably not.

Before I committed to DIY preparation, I remember getting full-job quotes in the ballpark of $12,000. Doing the prep myself brought the cash cost down dramatically. My recollection of the all-in figure, including paint, roughly $2,000 in painter labour and miscellaneous costs, is closer to $5,000.

But the bigger cost was time. I put an enormous number of hours into a relatively modest-value sedan. If I had simply sold the damaged car and added the money I spent on restoration, I could probably have bought a better E30 to begin with – perhaps even the coupe I would have preferred.

That is the rational answer.

The less rational answer is that I clearly enjoy projects. I tend to buy the cheaper car and convince myself I can fix it. Sometimes that is me being a cheapskate; sometimes it is genuinely because I like learning and like the challenge. This E30 was probably both.

## How it held up

The part I worried about most was the amount of filler used to repair the hail damage. I expected that years of heat and sun might eventually reveal cracks or movement.

So far, it has held up remarkably well. The major filled panels are still fine. The paint itself has survived well too, aside from the orange peel and cosmetic finishing that I would still like to improve.

The main body issue that has returned is the rust around the boot-seal area – the place where I now know the original repair did not get deeply enough into the seam. In 2026 I am repairing that properly by opening the area up and drilling spot welds where necessary.

There is also a recent bit of bad luck to address before the car is sold. A friend was staying with me while moving from Mackay to Sydney and accidentally backed his truck/trailer setup into the E30. Thankfully the damage was minor. I still have spare Glacier Blue basecoat left – probably around a litre or two – so I can repair that local section without having to start from scratch on colour matching.

## What I would do differently

If I somehow woke up tomorrow with another hail-damaged E30 sedan in the driveway, I do not think I would repeat the project exactly.

I would start with a better car. I would spend more upfront instead of automatically choosing the cheapest base. I would work in a proper enclosed garage. I would buy the good sanding and dust-extraction equipment before the first panel. I would use more metal/PDR work to minimise filler where practical. I would use a deliberate high-build-primer and block-sanding stage before colour. I would choose OEM body pieces instead of fighting aftermarket skirts and lips. And I would be more selective about the painter and the final clear-coat finish.

Most importantly, I would budget time more realistically. The job is not “sand the car and paint it”. The painting is almost the short part. The work is disassembly, cleaning, repair, rust, filler, sanding, inspection, primer, sanding again, masking, logistics and then carefully putting every tiny piece back without scratching the new finish.

Would I do another project like it? Probably – which suggests I did not learn every lesson.

But I would do the next one much smarter.

This car started as a $4,000 daily driver, got smashed by the 2021 Elizabeth hailstorm, was written off, almost sold, then spent the better part of a year being slowly rebuilt in a cramped West Croydon courtyard. It was not the cheapest way to own an E30 and it was definitely not the easiest. But I came out of it with a car I was proud of – and a much better idea of what is actually hiding underneath a good paint job.
